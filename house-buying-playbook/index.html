Welcome back to the market

Buying a house is a negotiation with six counterparties at once

The seller, the lender, the agent, the inspector's findings, the appraiser and the insurer each get a vote on price and terms. This guide walks the whole process in order, explains it plainly, and flags where the rules have changed since the last time you did this.

Coach's opening note

Housing is a rate × price problem, much like cloud is rate × usage. The purchase price is negotiated once; the mortgage rate compounds for thirty years. Buyers obsess over the first and accept the second from whichever lender their agent suggested. On a $500k loan, the gap between the best and the worst lender quote on the same day is routinely $40–80k of interest over the life of the loan. Shop the money as hard as you shop the house.

What's changed since you last bought

🤝 Agent commissions are unbundled Aug 2024

After the NAR settlement you sign a written buyer agreement before touring, and the seller's MLS listing can no longer promise your agent a fee. What your agent earns, and who pays it, is now a line you negotiate. §06

📈 Money costs more 7.03% · Sept 24 2026

Freddie Mac's 30-year average crossed 7% in late September 2026 for the first time since January 2025. Most sellers are sitting on 3–4% loans, which keeps inventory tight and makes assumable loans and seller-paid buydowns worth real money. §05

🔥 Insurance can kill a deal

Premiums have jumped sharply, and in wildfire, hurricane and hail country some carriers have stopped writing new policies. Get a bindable quote during the inspection period, not the week before closing. §09

🎣 Wire fraud is the #1 closing risk

Criminals watch real-estate email threads and send "updated wiring instructions" hours before closing. That money is almost never recovered. Call a number you already know to verify every wire. §10

📵 Fewer spam calls after you apply Mar 2026

The Homebuyers Privacy Protection Act largely ended "trigger leads", where credit bureaus sold the news of your mortgage inquiry to competing lenders. Shopping several lenders no longer brings on a flood of calls.

🧾 Tax rules settled 2025 law

The 2025 tax act made the $750k mortgage-interest cap permanent, brought back the PMI deduction from 2026, and raised the SALT cap to $40k through 2029 (phased down at high incomes). §14

Foundations

The game plan & your team

A purchase is a relay race with handoffs between six or seven professionals. Most delays and blown deals happen at the handoffs, so know who holds the baton at each stage.

The master timeline

3+ months out

Get the money straight

Pull all three credit reports, fix errors, and stop opening new credit. Decide your all-in monthly ceiling (§02). Gather two years of tax returns, W-2s and statements. Park your down payment somewhere safe and liquid.

6–10 weeks out

Pre-approval & lender shortlist

Get a fully documented pre-approval from one lender, plus same-day Loan Estimates from 2–3 others so you know the market (§05).

4–8 weeks out

Pick your agent, sign the buyer agreement

Interview two or three. Negotiate the fee, the term and the exit clause before you sign (§06).

Search

Tour, compare, research the area

Pull comps, insurance history and flood and fire maps for any serious candidate (§07).

Day 0

Offer accepted: under contract

Earnest money goes to escrow within 1–3 business days. The contingency clocks start now (§08).

Days 1–10

Inspection period

Inspections, HOA documents, insurance quote, title commitment review, then a repair request (§09).

Days 7–21

Appraisal & underwriting

The lender orders the appraisal. Underwriting asks for more documents, called "conditions". Lock your rate if you haven't already.

≥3 business days before close

Closing Disclosure

Compare it line by line with your Loan Estimate. Verify the wire instructions by phone.

Closing day

Final walk-through, sign, fund, record

You get keys once the deed records with the county, sometimes the same afternoon and sometimes the next day (§10).

Analogy: the deal team

Think of it like running an RFP with outside advisors. The agent is your deal lead, the lender is your financing partner, the inspector is technical due diligence, and title/escrow is the paying agent. Every one of them is paid when the deal closes, not when it closes well. You're the only person at the table paid by a good outcome, so you own the decision rights.

Watch out: the steered team

"Preferred" lenders, inspectors and title companies are sometimes a referral relationship or an affiliated business (disclosed on an Affiliated Business Arrangement form). They may still be good, but pick an inspector who has no reason to keep the agent happy, and always get outside lender quotes to compare.

The Money

Affordability & the true cost of owning

Lenders tell you the most you can borrow. That's a ceiling, not a target. Decide your own number first, from what you'd spend each month, and work backward to a price.

PITI: the payment lenders underwrite

Monthly housing = Principal + Interest + Taxes + Insurance + HOA + mortgage insurance

The lender collects taxes and insurance monthly into an escrow account (also called "impound") and pays the bills for you. Escrow is why your payment changes every year even on a fixed-rate loan: taxes and premiums move.

The ratios lenders use

Front-end ratio (housing ÷ gross income)

Classic guideline 28%. Automated underwriting often ignores it for conventional loans. It's still a good personal sanity check.

Back-end DTI (all debts ÷ gross income)

Housing plus car, student and card minimums plus any other mortgage or HELOC. Conventional loans go to 45–50% with strong files. FHA can stretch past 50%. VA uses residual income as well.

Coach's note: set your own ceiling

A comfortable number for most households: total housing at or below 25% of gross, or about 35% of take-home. Being approved at 45% DTI means the lender is comfortable, not you. Build the budget from your real spending, not from the pre-approval letter.

Payment estimator

🧮 What will this house cost per month?

—Principal & interest
—Est. PMI (if <20% down)
—Total monthly (PITI + HOA)
—Housing ÷ gross income
—Cash to close (down + ~3%)

PMI is estimated at 0.5%/yr of the loan, a rough mid-range for good credit; real pricing runs about 0.2–1.5% by score and down payment. Everything stays in your browser.

Watch out: the reassessment surprise

A seller who bought 15 years ago may pay half the tax you will, because many states cap assessment growth for existing owners, then reset it at sale. Some states send a supplemental tax bill months after closing. Ask the lender to escrow using the post-sale estimate, or keep a cushion yourself. (Arizona is an exception: its cap doesn't reset on sale, see §12.)

Rent vs. buy: the honest version

Buying wins when you stay long enough for appreciation and principal paydown to outrun the unrecoverable costs: interest, property tax, insurance, maintenance, and the 6–10% round-trip cost of buying and later selling. At 7% rates, break-even is often 5–7+ years. Renting's unrecoverable costs are rent plus renter's insurance. The fair comparison sets unrecoverable cost against unrecoverable cost, not rent against the full mortgage payment.

Annual unrecoverable cost of owning ≈ price × (interest rate × LTV + tax rate + ~1% maintenance + cost of capital on equity)
The Money

Credit & pre-approval

Your credit score sets your rate tier and your mortgage-insurance price. A documented pre-approval tells sellers you can actually close. Do both before you tour a single house.

Credit: what moves the price

Lenders traditionally use the middle of three bureau scores, using older "classic" FICO models. FHFA has approved FICO 10T and VantageScore 4.0 for Fannie and Freddie loans and is phasing them in, so your lender's model may differ from the score in your banking app.

  • Pull all three reports free at AnnualCreditReport.com and dispute errors now. Disputes can take 30+ days, and an open dispute can stall underwriting.
  • Pay card balances down before the statement date, not the due date. Reported utilization under ~10% scores best.
  • Rate-shop in a tight window. Mortgage inquiries within 14–45 days (by scoring model) count as one.
  • Open nothing new: no new cards, car loans or co-signing until after closing. Lenders re-pull credit or monitor it right up to funding.
  • If your credit is frozen, temporarily lift the freeze at all three bureaus before you apply.

The document pack to assemble now

📄 Income

30 days of pay stubs, 2 years of W-2s and full tax returns (all schedules), 1099s. Self-employed: 2 years of business returns plus a year-to-date P&L. Bonus or RSU income usually needs a 2-year history to count. VA disability award letter if applicable (it's non-taxable, and lenders may "gross it up" by 25%).

🏦 Assets

Two months of complete statements (every page, even the blank ones) for every account you'll use. Explain any large non-payroll deposit. A gift needs a signed gift letter and a paper trail.

🏠 Existing property

Current mortgage statement, HELOC statement, homeowner's declarations page, tax bill, HOA statement. The lender counts your full current housing cost in DTI.

🪪 Identity & history

ID, Social Security number, 2 years of address and employment history, divorce decree and child-support orders if relevant.

Watch out: "seasoned" money

Lenders need to source any large deposit in the last 60 days. Moving money between your own accounts is fine but creates paperwork. Selling stock or crypto for a down payment needs a clean trail showing the sale and the transfer. Move funds early and in as few hops as possible, ideally before the two statement months the lender will review.

Coach's note: the assumable-loan play

FHA, VA and USDA loans are assumable. A seller with a 2.75% VA loan from 2021 is sitting on a financing asset worth tens of thousands of dollars. Search listings for "assumable". The gap between price and loan balance has to be covered in cash or with a second lien. If a non-veteran assumes a VA loan, the seller's VA entitlement stays tied up, so veteran sellers often prefer a veteran buyer who can substitute their own entitlement.

Fixed vs. adjustable in a 7% world

Choose a 30-year fixed when…

You might stay 8+ years, you value certainty, or you'd struggle if the payment rose. You keep the option to refinance if rates fall, and pay nothing for it except closing costs.

Consider a 7/6 or 10/6 ARM when…

The ARM is priced 0.5%+ below the fixed rate, and you're confident you'll sell, pay down or refinance inside the fixed window. Stress-test the payment at the lifetime cap.

Choose a 15-year when…

You can comfortably handle the bigger payment and want the lower rate (often 0.5–0.75% below the 30). Or take a 30 and prepay voluntarily, which keeps the flexibility.

Avoid…

Interest-only or negative-amortization products, and prepayment penalties on a home you live in. Also avoid stretching to a price where only an ARM makes the payment work.

Analogy: the fixed rate is a free option

A fixed-rate mortgage is a one-way contract in your favor. If rates rise you keep your rate forever. If they fall you refinance and walk away. The lender can't do either. That's why today's sellers won't give up their 3% loans, and why "marry the house, date the rate" is only half-right: the date comes with closing costs of 1–3%.

Watch out: occupancy fraud

Primary, second-home and investment loans are priced differently, and you sign an occupancy affidavit. Calling a rental a "second home" to get the better rate is mortgage fraud, a federal crime, even when "everyone does it". Pick the loan that matches how you'll actually use the house. §11 covers the line between a second home and an investment property.

The Money

Rates, points & lender shopping

This is the part of the deal most like your day job. The price is a rate-and-fee pair, quotes vary widely for the same borrower on the same day, and the standard disclosure form makes like-for-like comparison possible.

Read the Loan Estimate like a bid sheet

Every lender must give you a standardized 3-page Loan Estimate within 3 business days of an application (name, income, SSN, address, price and loan amount). Page 2 is where the money is.

SectionWhat it isNegotiable?
A · Origination chargesLender's fee, points, underwriting and processingYes, the main lever. Compare A alongside the rate.
B · Services you cannot shop forAppraisal, credit report, flood certificationLittle. Mostly pass-through.
C · Services you can shop forTitle, settlement and survey feesYes. Shop title where your state allows.
E · Taxes & government feesRecording, transfer taxesNo. Fixed by law.
F / G · Prepaids & initial escrowFirst-year insurance, prepaid interest, escrow cushionNo. Identical across lenders, so ignore them when comparing.
Coach's note: running the competitive bid

Request Loan Estimates from 3–4 lenders on the same morning with the same lock period (say 45 days) and the same loan type. Include one big bank, one credit union, one online lender and one local broker. Compare rate + Section A. Then tell your favorite what the best competing quote was and send the PDF. Lenders price-match regularly, and a written competing quote does more than any verbal "can you do better?"

Points: buying down the rate

1 point = 1% of the loan amount · Break-even months = cost of points ÷ monthly payment savings

One point typically lowers the rate by roughly 0.125–0.25%, though it varies daily by lender. Points pay off only if you keep the loan past break-even, and in a 7% market many people will refinance within a few years. Lender credits run the other way: a higher rate in exchange for cash toward closing costs.

🧮 Are the points worth it?

—Cost of points
—Monthly P&I savings
—Break-even
—Net gain over your horizon

Simple break-even ignores the time value of the upfront cash and the slightly faster principal paydown at a lower rate. Both roughly offset over short horizons.

Rate locks

  • Lock once you're under contract for a lock period that clears your closing date with a week to spare. 30/45/60-day locks cost progressively more.
  • Get the lock confirmation in writing: rate, points or credits, expiry, loan amount and program.
  • Ask about a float-down, a one-time re-price if rates drop more than a threshold. It's sometimes free and sometimes 0.25–0.5 point.
  • Know the extension cost. Closing delays happen, and extensions are commonly priced per day.
  • A lock is tied to the file. Changing loan amount, program, occupancy or credit can change the price.

Seller-paid buydowns: the concession that beats a price cut

Temporary buydown (2-1, 1-0)

The seller funds an escrow that cuts your rate by 2% in year 1 and 1% in year 2, before it returns to the note rate. You still qualify at the full note rate. Useful if you expect to refinance within two years anyway.

Permanent buydown

Seller credit used to buy discount points on your loan. It lasts the life of the loan, or until you refinance.

Analogy: price vs. terms

It's the same move as trading list-price discount for payment terms in a vendor deal. A $15k price cut on a $500k house with 20% down trims the loan by $12k and saves about $80/month at 7%. The same $15k as a permanent buydown can cut the rate by roughly 0.5–0.75%, around $130–200/month on a $400k loan. The seller's net is identical. Run both before you counter.

Watch out: concession caps

Seller credits are capped as a percentage of price. Conventional primary: 3% under 10% down, 6% with 10–25% down, 9% above 25%. Second homes and investment properties: 2%. FHA 6%. VA 4% plus normal closing costs. A credit can't exceed your actual closing costs, and anything over the cap is lost.

The Hunt

Agents & the new commission rules

The biggest change since you last bought. Your agent's pay used to arrive invisibly from the seller's side. Now it's a contract you sign and a number you negotiate.

What the 2024 NAR settlement changed

✍️ Written agreement before touring

MLS-participating agents must have a signed agreement with you before you tour a home, in person or virtually. It must state the compensation as a specific amount or rate, not "whatever the seller offers", and the agent can't collect more than that.

🚫 No compensation offers on the MLS

Listings can't advertise what the seller pays the buyer's agent. Sellers can still agree to pay it, negotiated directly or requested in your offer as a concession.

💵 Who pays is now a term of the deal

Your agent's fee might come from the seller (via concession or listing-agent split), from you at closing, or both. Your offer should say what you're asking for.

🔍 Everything is negotiable, always was

The fee rate, a flat fee, the term, which homes it covers, and how you can cancel.

Coach's note: interview like it's an RFP

Ask each candidate: How many buyers did you close in this area and price band last year? Walk me through how you'd price the last house you lost to another offer. What's your fee, and what do I get at a lower one? Who covers showings when you're away? Good agents answer with comps and specifics. Weak ones answer with enthusiasm.

Alternatives to the full-service agent

Flat-fee / discount

A lower fixed fee, sometimes with less hands-on touring. Works well if you do your own search and comps.

Rebate brokerages

Refund part of their fee to you at closing (lender approval required). Some states restrict or ban rebates, so check yours.

Unrepresented + attorney

Deal directly with the listing agent and hire a real-estate attorney for the contract. Cheapest. The listing agent is not working for you.

Watch out: the listing agent is the seller's advocate

Anything you tell a listing agent, like your maximum, your timeline or how much you love the kitchen, can reach the seller. When you're unrepresented, act as if every call is on speakerphone with the seller.

The Hunt

Finding & judging a house

Fall for the location, fix the house. A dated kitchen is a weekend of quotes; a floodplain, a failing HOA or an uninsurable fire zone are permanent.

Write the scorecard before you tour

Non-negotiables

Location limits, bedrooms, single-level or not, lot size, commute, insurability. Houses that fail these don't get toured.

Weighted wants

Garage, view, office, yard, updated systems. Give each a weight and score every tour the same evening.

Fixables

Paint, floors, fixtures, landscaping. Price them in; don't let them decide for you.

Build your own comps

Comparable = sold (not listed) · within ~0.5 mi or same subdivision · last 3–6 months · ±20% sq ft · same era and style

Take 3–6 comps, adjust for real differences (garage, pool, updated kitchen, lot, view), and compare price per square foot as a check rather than the answer. The appraiser will do almost exactly this, so your comps predict whether the house will appraise.

Area due diligence, before you offer

  • Visit at 7:30am on a weekday, 9pm on a Friday and Saturday midday. Traffic, noise, trains, parking, barking dogs.
  • Flood: FEMA flood map (msc.fema.gov) plus a risk-score site like First Street. Lenders require flood insurance in high-risk zones, and NFIP policies have a 30-day wait.
  • Wildfire: state fire-hazard maps and the US Forest Service's Wildfire Risk to Communities. Ask an insurance broker whether they can write it before you offer.
  • Insurance history: ask the seller for a CLUE report showing prior claims on the property. Prior water claims raise premiums.
  • Permits: check the city or county permit portal. Unpermitted additions or garage conversions can mean re-work, appraisal problems and insurance gaps.
  • What's coming: the planning department's agenda for rezoning, road widening or large developments nearby.
  • HOA health (§09): reserves, special assessments, litigation, rental caps, rules on RVs, paint and short-term rentals.
  • Utilities & services: internet providers and speeds at the address, sewer or septic, city water or well, trash, propane.

The negotiator's corner

🧭 Know your walk-away before you see the house again

Set three numbers: opening, target and walk-away. Write the walk-away down. Emotional overbidding happens in the 20 minutes after your agent says "there's another offer".

🎯 Anchor with evidence

A below-list offer with a one-page comps sheet attached reads as a serious buyer. Without the comps it reads as a lowball.

🔁 Trade, don't concede

Every give should get something back: a higher price for a seller-paid buydown, a faster close for a lower price, or a rent-back for dropped repair requests.

📉 Your BATNA is the market

Keep touring until you're under contract, and let the listing agent see that you have other options. Stale listings (§07) are where leverage lives.

Coach's note: attend the inspection

Spend the last hour with the inspector. Ask "what would you fix first if this were your house?" and "which of these is normal for a house this age?" The written report lists everything with equal weight, and 40 pages of findings can make a good house look scary. The conversation tells you which three items actually matter.

The repair request: repair, credit or price?

💵 Credit at closing

Usually best. You control the contractor and the quality. Limited by the concession caps (§05) and your actual closing costs.

📉 Price reduction

Lowers the loan, taxes and down payment slightly. Good when the credit would exceed the cap. Requires a contract amendment, and the lender will update.

🔧 Seller repairs

The seller picks the cheapest contractor. If you go this way, require licensed trades, permits where required and receipts, and re-inspect at the walk-through.

Ask for safety, structure, water and big-system items with contractor quotes attached. Don't ask for cosmetics or routine maintenance: that burns goodwill and invites a "no" on everything.

The paperwork diligence running in parallel

  • Seller disclosure: read every "yes" and every "unknown". Ask what was done about each.
  • Title commitment: review exceptions such as easements, CC&Rs and encroachments. Ask title about anything odd and order a survey if boundaries matter.
  • HOA packet: budget, reserve study (under ~70% funded is a warning), 12 months of minutes, special assessments, litigation, rental restrictions, transfer fees.
  • Insurance: a bindable quote, not a ballpark. Confirm roof age limits, wind/hail deductible, wildfire exclusions and whether flood coverage is required.
  • Permits: compare permitted square footage and additions against the listing.
  • Leases, solar and service contracts that transfer to you: propane tank lease, security system, solar PPA.
Watch out: insurance is now a deal-breaker, not a formality

In wildfire, coastal and hail-heavy markets, standard carriers may decline based on fire score, roof age or claims history. The fallback is a surplus-lines carrier or the state FAIR plan, often at 2–4× the cost with thinner coverage. Get the quote during the inspection period so you can still exit. Premium swings of several thousand dollars a year change what you can afford (§02).

The Deal

Appraisal, underwriting & closing

The home stretch is mostly waiting and answering document requests quickly. Three things can still go wrong: a low appraisal, a change in your finances, and wire fraud.

The appraisal

The lender lends on the lower of price or appraised value. If a $500k contract appraises at $480k, your 20%-down math now runs off $480k, and the $20k gap has to be bridged.

Options on a low appraisal

(1) Seller cuts the price. (2) You cover the gap in cash. (3) Split it. (4) Challenge it with a Reconsideration of Value (ROV) through the lender, with better comps. (5) Exit under the appraisal contingency.

Appraisal waivers

Fannie and Freddie sometimes offer to skip the appraisal (or accept a property data collection) on low-LTV loans. That saves time and fee, but you lose the independent value check.

Underwriting: the do-not list

Watch out: freeze your financial life until funding

Until the loan funds, don't change jobs or pay structure, open or close credit, finance furniture, co-sign, make large undocumented deposits, move money between accounts without a paper trail, or pay off debts without asking your loan officer first. Lenders re-verify employment and often re-pull credit days before closing.

Expect a list of "conditions": explain a deposit, re-send a statement page, sign a letter about a credit inquiry. Turn each around the same day. Most late closings are waiting on the borrower, not the lender.

The Closing Disclosure (CD)

You must receive the CD at least 3 business days before closing. Compare it with your final Loan Estimate:

  • Rate, loan amount, term, loan type match your lock.
  • Section A (origination) has not increased. Lender fees have zero tolerance.
  • Section B plus recording fees have not risen more than 10% in total, if you used the lender's providers.
  • Credits are all there: seller concessions, lender credits, repair credits, earnest money.
  • Cash to close matches your own estimate within a few hundred dollars (prorations of tax and HOA explain the rest).

A new 3-day wait is triggered only if the APR rises beyond tolerance, the loan product changes, or a prepayment penalty is added. Other corrections just produce a revised CD.

Moving the money safely

Watch out: wire fraud (read this twice)

Criminals compromise agent, title or buyer email and send convincing "updated wiring instructions", often the day before closing. Before any wire: call the title company at a phone number from its official website or your original contract, never one in the email, and read back the account and routing numbers. Title companies don't change wire instructions by email. If money goes to the wrong place, call your bank to recall the wire within minutes and file at ic3.gov. Ask about a cashier's check or an ACH/escrow portal if they're offered.

Closing day

24 hrs before (or the morning of)

Final walk-through

Agreed repairs done (with receipts), included items present, no new damage, systems running, seller's belongings gone. Run every faucet and appliance.

Signing

Sign the note, deed of trust or mortgage, and disclosures

About an hour. In-person, hybrid, or fully remote online notarization (RON) where your state and lender allow. Bring ID and ask about anything you don't understand.

Funding

Lender wires the loan; escrow disburses

Sometimes same day. In "dry funding" states, funding and keys can come a day or two after signing.

Recording

Deed recorded with the county → keys

Get digital copies of the full signed package and the final settlement statement. You'll need them at tax time and when you sell.

Analogy: escrow states vs. attorney states

Western states (AZ, CA, NV, WA and others) mostly close through a neutral escrow officer at a title company, and you may never sit at the same table as the seller. Much of the East uses attorney closings, where a lawyer conducts the signing. Same outcome, different conductor. Know which one your state uses so you know who to call.

Special situations

Second homes & tapping equity

Buying while you already own changes almost everything: which loan you qualify for, how you fund the down payment, what's tax-deductible and how the house gets insured. Most of the expensive mistakes here are structural, not about price.

Second home or investment property? The lender decides by use

🏔 Second home

A one-unit home you occupy for part of the year, suitable for year-round use, under your exclusive control. No rental-pool or management agreement that dictates occupancy. Occasional rental is generally tolerated, but rental income can't be used to qualify. Down payment 10%+, seller concessions capped at 2%, pricing slightly above primary, reserves required.

🏘 Investment property

Bought mainly to rent. Down payment 15–25%, noticeably higher rate, more reserves. Projected rent (appraiser's rent schedule, or DSCR loans) can help you qualify.

Watch out: VA and FHA loans don't fit here

Both are for primary residences. A vacation or weekend home needs conventional, jumbo or portfolio financing, or cash. (VA can finance a new primary while you keep and rent out the old one, if you have remaining entitlement. That's a relocation, not a second home.)

The blended-rate test

When you have a cheap first mortgage, compare the blended cost of keeping it plus a second lien against the single rate of a cash-out refi:

Blended rate = (balance₁ × rate₁ + balance₂ × rate₂) ÷ (balance₁ + balance₂)

Example with round numbers: $250k at 3.0% plus a $150k HELOC at 8.0% blends to 4.88%, far below a 7% cash-out refi on the full $400k. Flip the proportions, with a small cheap first mortgage and a large new need, and the blend climbs toward the second-lien rate while the HELOC still floats. Run the numbers rather than assuming.

Taxes: where second homes differ

Interest follows the money and the collateral

Mortgage interest is deductible only on debt used to buy, build or substantially improve the home that secures it, up to $750k of combined acquisition debt across your main and second home. A HELOC on house A spent on house B generally doesn't qualify. IRS rules let a mortgage taken on house B within 90 days after purchase count as acquisition debt (to the extent of the purchase cost), which is one more reason the "cash now, finance later" route can beat a long-lived HELOC.

No $250k/$500k exclusion on sale

The home-sale gain exclusion applies to your main home (lived in 2 of the last 5 years). A second home's gain is taxable. Converting it to your primary later only partly helps, because "nonqualified use" periods since 2009 are prorated out.

Property tax classification

Many states give primary residences a homestead exemption, assessment cap or rebate that a second home doesn't get. Arizona's homeowner rebate on school taxes is one example (§12). Budget the second home's taxes at the non-primary rate.

Renting it out

Rent it 14 days or fewer a year and the income is tax-free (and unreported). More than that, and expenses split between personal and rental days, with loss limits if your personal use exceeds 14 days or 10% of rental days.

Tax treatment depends on facts: how funds are traced, when debt was incurred, rental days. Confirm your structure with a CPA before you close, not at filing time.

Owning from a distance

  • Short-term rental rules: city permits, state lodging or sales-tax licenses (Arizona requires a TPT license, for example, see §12), HOA bans, and whether your loan allows it. Check before you buy, not after.
  • Insurance: tell the insurer it's a secondary or seasonal home. Standard policies can restrict coverage after 30–60 days of vacancy, and short-term rental needs its own endorsement or policy.
  • Water is the #1 remote-owner loss: leak sensors, an automatic main shut-off valve, a smart thermostat with freeze alerts, and winterizing if you're in freeze country.
  • A local bench: handyman, HVAC company, plumber, and someone to check the house after storms. Line them up at the inspection.
  • Wildfire country: defensible space (0–5 ft ember-resistant, 5–30 ft lean and clean), ember-resistant vents, and Firewise community status can make the difference to insurability and premium.

How the Arizona contract runs

Most resale deals use the Arizona Association of REALTORS® (AAR) Residential Resale Real Estate Purchase Contract. It's an escrow state: a title company's escrow officer holds the earnest money, clears title and runs a closing where buyer and seller usually sign separately. No attorney is required. Many buyers hire one anyway for rural land, well-share agreements or odd title.

Day 0

Acceptance

Earnest money (often 1–2%) goes to escrow. The seller owes you the SPDS (Seller's Property Disclosure Statement) and a 5-year insurance claims history within days, so read both inside your inspection period.

Days 1–10

Inspection period (the "10" in 10/5/5)

You can cancel for essentially any reason by delivering a BINSR (Buyer's Inspection Notice and Seller's Response) before day 10. Book inspectors before you offer. Aim to send the BINSR by day 6–7.

+5 days

Seller responds

The seller agrees, refuses or counters on your repair list. Silence counts as a refusal.

+5 days

Your decision

If the seller didn't agree to everything, you can accept what was offered or cancel with your earnest money. Miss this window and you've accepted what's on the table.

Day ~10 onward

Loan Status Update, appraisal, HOA docs

Your lender sends an LSU form to the seller's side. There's a short window to cancel after a low appraisal notice, and after receiving the HOA resale disclosure.

Close of escrow (COE)

Walk-through, sign, record

Keys at recording, often the same day. Arizona uses deeds of trust.

Watch out: the clock doesn't wait for your inspector

Deadlines in the AAR contract are strict, and "I was waiting on the roofer" doesn't extend them. In a busy season good inspectors book a week out. If the calendar is tight, write a longer inspection period (14 days) into your offer rather than hoping.

Money: what's cheaper, and what's different

🚫 No transfer tax

The Arizona Constitution bans real-estate transfer taxes. You pay a nominal recording fee and file an Affidavit of Property Value. Closing costs run lighter than in the Northeast.

🧾 Who pays for what (custom)

Seller usually pays the owner's title policy. Buyer pays the lender's policy. Escrow fees are commonly split 50/50. All of it is negotiable in the contract.

📊 Property tax: the cap travels with the house

Tax is levied on the Limited Property Value, which can rise at most 5% a year and doesn't reset when the house sells. That's the opposite of the reassessment warning in §02. The seller's current bill is a fair guide, unless the house was remodeled or changes use.

🏠 Primary vs. second home classification

Owner-occupied homes are Class 3 and get the state's Homeowner Rebate on school taxes (up to $600/yr), with primary taxes capped at 1% of LPV. Second homes and rentals are Class 4 and get neither. Tick the right box on the Affidavit of Property Value. Rentals must also be registered with the county assessor.

📅 Taxes are paid in arrears

First half is due Oct 1 (delinquent after Nov 1), second half Mar 1 (delinquent after May 1). At closing the seller credits you for their share of the bill you'll pay later. Check the proration on your Closing Disclosure.

💍 Community property state

If you're married, a spouse generally has to sign on the purchase, loan and deed even if only one of you is on the loan. Ask escrow about vesting as "community property with right of survivorship", which avoids probate on the first death and has tax-basis advantages. Worth a quick question to an estate attorney.

Arizona-flavored inspection list

🔥 Wildfire & insurance

Arizona has no FAIR plan for homes, so there's no state insurer of last resort. In forested areas like Prescott, Payson and Flagstaff, if admitted carriers decline, the fallback is the excess & surplus lines market at a higher price. Get a bindable quote in the first days of the inspection period. Defensible space and Firewise community status help.

🐜 Termites & scorpions

Subterranean termites are common, and a WDIIR (wood-destroying insect inspection report) is standard and required by FHA and VA. Ask about bark scorpions in the desert; quarterly pest control is normal.

🏚 Roofs, heat & cooling

Tile roofs look fine for decades while the underlayment beneath fails (often at 20–25 years), so get it looked at. Foam flat roofs need recoating every few years. Desert heat shortens HVAC life. At elevation, check for evaporative coolers and pipe freeze protection.

⛰ Soil, fissures & washes

Expansive clay soils crack slabs and stucco, so call a structural engineer on cracks. In parts of Maricopa, Pinal and Cochise counties, check the Arizona Geological Survey earth fissure maps. Monsoon storms send water down dry washes, so check FEMA maps and drainage around the house.

☀️ Solar

Very common, and often leased or financed. Get the contract, transfer terms, remaining payments and any UCC filing early, because the solar company's approval can take weeks.

🏊 Pools

Sellers must give you the state's pool-safety notice. Check the barrier (fence and gate) rules if kids will visit, along with equipment age and resurfacing.

Rural land, HOAs & renting it out

  • Unincorporated parcels: sellers of land outside city limits owe an Affidavit of Disclosure covering legal access, road maintenance, utilities, flood zone and septic suitability. Legal access is not the same as a dirt road that happens to exist.
  • HOA resale disclosure: the association must deliver its disclosure packet (fees, assessments, violations, litigation) within about 10 days. You get a short window to cancel after receiving it.
  • Short-term rentals: state law bars cities from banning STRs outright, but since 2022 cities can require permits, emergency contacts and neighbor notification (Sedona, Prescott, Phoenix and others do). Every STR needs a state TPT license shown on its listings. HOA CC&Rs can still prohibit them, so read those first.
  • Contractors: check licenses at the Registrar of Contractors (ROC). Licensed contractors are backed by a state recovery fund, and unlicensed handyman work is limited to small jobs.

Protections worth knowing

🛡 Homestead exemption (creditor)

Arizona automatically protects roughly $400k of equity in your primary residence from most creditors. No filing needed. It covers your primary home only, not a second home.

⚖️ Anti-deficiency rules

A purchase-money loan on a one- or two-family home on 2.5 acres or less is generally protected from a deficiency judgment after foreclosure. Refinances and HELOCs can lose that protection. It's fact-specific, so ask an attorney if it affects your financing choice.

Coach's note: the Arizona short list

Before you offer on anything outside a city: (1) where does the water come from and is it documented, (2) can an admitted carrier insure it, (3) what does the septic report say, and (4) is there recorded legal access. Before you close: tick primary vs. second home correctly on the affidavit, and calendar the ADEQ and ADWR filings for the week after. Buying around Prescott? The local layer is in §13.

Jurisdiction decides the rules

The same street name can run through the City of Prescott, unincorporated Yavapai County and another town. The parcel's jurisdiction decides:

🏛 Permits & septic

Building permits come from the city or town, or from Yavapai County Development Services if unincorporated. Septic records and the Notice of Transfer go through the county's Environmental Services Unit via its online portal, within 15 days of closing (§12).

🚒 Fire service & insurance class

Inside the city it's Prescott Fire; much of the surrounding area is covered by the regional fire authority. Insurers price partly on the ISO Public Protection Class, and much of rural Yavapai rates 9 or 10 (far from a station or a hydrant). That can move a premium as much as the fire score does.

🏘 Short-term rentals

Each city and town has its own code: City of Prescott, Prescott Valley, and county rules elsewhere. See below.

💧 Water provider

City of Prescott, Town of Prescott Valley, a private water company, or your own well. Each has a different long-term supply story.

Wildfire & insurance: do this first

Watch out: this is fire country

The area has lived through the 2013 Yarnell Hill fire (19 of Prescott's Granite Mountain Hotshots died), the 2013 Doce fire and the 2017 Goodwin fire. Insurers price that history in. Premiums in the wildland-urban interface have kept climbing, carriers have non-renewed policies in higher-risk ZIPs, and Arizona has no FAIR plan (§12). Get a bindable quote for the specific address before you offer, or in the first two days of the inspection period at the latest.

  • Ask the broker for the carrier's wildfire score on the address, not only the premium. It tells you whether renewal is at risk.
  • Check USFS Wildfire Risk to Communities and the parcel's distance to continuous pine. The Prescott Home Tour planner already shows Forest Service wildfire hazard for each house on the tour.
  • Look at the house the way an ember would: Class A roof, needles in gutters, wood decks and fences touching the house, unscreened vents, junipers against the walls.
  • Firewise community? Yavapai Firewise works with neighborhood associations on fuel reduction. Membership and recent mitigation help with some insurers. Prescott Fire's wildfire risk program has guidance for homeowners.
  • Two ways out. Drive the evacuation route from a cul-de-sac in the pines and time it. Sign up for Yavapai County emergency alerts at the address.

Water in the Prescott AMA

City of Prescott water

The Prescott Active Management Area was declared out of safe yield in 1999, meaning groundwater pumping exceeds recharge. The City of Prescott holds a 100-year assured water supply designation, the only provider in the AMA with one. More supply depends on importing Big Chino groundwater, which needs a pipeline not yet built. For an existing home with a city meter, that's context. For a vacant lot or a split, confirm water service before you buy.

Private wells (Chino, Williamson Valley, Dewey-Humboldt)

Pull the well log from ADWR's well registry (Wells55) for drilled depth, original static water level and pump setting, and compare with today's reading from the well inspection. A falling level in a shallow well is a future re-drill. Test for arsenic and uranium/radium, which occur naturally in the area's granite. Then file ADWR form 55-71A after closing (§12).

Ownership

After the keys

Closing ends the transaction, not the job. The first 90 days set up your records, your protections and your maintenance habits.

First week

  • Re-key or replace every exterior lock; reset garage openers, keypad codes and any smart locks, cameras and thermostats (remove the seller's accounts).
  • Find the main water shut-off, gas shut-off and electrical panel, and label the breakers.
  • Test or replace smoke and CO detectors, change HVAC filters and note the water-heater date.
  • Transfer utilities, internet, trash and propane; set up mail forwarding.
  • Make a home file (digital and paper): closing package, deed, inspection report, warranties, manuals, paint colors, receipts for improvements.

First 90 days

  • File for any homestead exemption or primary-residence classification your state offers. It's often not automatic, and deadlines are strict.
  • Sign up for your county recorder's property-fraud alert (free in many counties). It emails you if any document is recorded against your property.
  • Watch for your loan being sold. Servicing transfers are normal and come with official notices from both the old and new servicer. Verify the new servicer independently before sending payments.
  • Ignore the scam mail: "certified deed copy" offers for $80+, "mortgage protection" life insurance, and fake "escrow shortage" notices. Your deed is free or a few dollars from the county.
  • Check your first escrow statement, especially if taxes will reset to your purchase price (§02).

Shrinking the payment later

Drop PMI

Request removal at 80% of original value. It ends automatically at 78%. With appreciation, a new appraisal can get you there sooner (typically 75% LTV after 2 years, 80% after 5).

Recast

Pay a lump sum, and many servicers re-amortize to a lower payment for a small fee (~$250), keeping your rate. Not available on FHA or VA.

Refinance

Worth a look when rates fall ~0.75–1%+ below yours. Check break-even with the points formula (§05) and include the cost of restarting the term.

Coach's note: fund it like a sinking fund

Set aside 1–2% of the home's value each year into a separate account, weighted by the ages of the roof, HVAC and water heater from your inspection report. When the water heater dies in year four, it becomes a withdrawal instead of an emergency. Home warranties rarely beat this: they cap payouts, exclude "pre-existing" and "improper maintenance" failures, and choose the contractor.

Formulas

Monthly P&I = L × r(1+r)ⁿ ÷ ((1+r)ⁿ − 1)  ·  r = annual rate ÷ 12, n = months
DTI = all monthly debt payments (incl. new PITI) ÷ gross monthly income
LTV = loan ÷ lower of price or appraisal  ·  CLTV = all liens ÷ value
Points break-even (months) = points cost ÷ monthly P&I savings
Blended rate = Σ(balance × rate) ÷ Σ balance
Rule of thumb: every $1,000 borrowed at 7% for 30 yrs ≈ $6.65/month

Glossary

Amortization
Paying a loan down on a schedule; early payments are mostly interest, later ones mostly principal.
APR
Annual percentage rate: the interest rate plus certain fees, expressed as a yearly rate. Useful for comparison when the loan is held to term.
Appraisal gap
The difference between contract price and a lower appraised value, which someone has to cover or negotiate away.
ARM
Adjustable-rate mortgage. "7/6" means fixed for 7 years, then adjusting every 6 months, bounded by caps.
Assumable loan
A mortgage (FHA, VA, USDA) a qualified buyer can take over with its existing rate and balance.
BATNA
Best alternative to a negotiated agreement: in house hunting, the next-best house or simply waiting.
Bindable quote
An insurance quote the carrier will actually issue on, as opposed to an estimate.
Buyer representation agreement
The written contract with your agent (required before touring since August 2024) that sets their duties and pay.
Buydown
Paying upfront to reduce the interest rate, temporarily (2-1, 1-0) or permanently (discount points).
Cash to close
Down payment plus closing costs and prepaids, minus deposits and credits: the final wire.
CC&Rs
Covenants, conditions and restrictions: rules recorded against the property, usually enforced by an HOA.
Closing Disclosure (CD)
The 5-page final statement of loan terms and costs, due at least 3 business days before closing.
CLUE report
A database record of insurance claims on a property over the past 5–7 years.
Conforming loan
A loan within FHFA limits ($832,750 baseline in 2026) that Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac can buy.
Contingency
A contract condition that lets you exit (usually with your deposit) if something doesn't happen: inspection, financing, appraisal, insurance, sale.
Delayed financing
Taking a mortgage shortly after buying with cash, treated like a purchase loan.
DTI
Debt-to-income ratio. Monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income.
Earnest money (EMD)
The good-faith deposit held in escrow and credited at closing.
Escrow
(1) A neutral party holding funds and documents until closing. (2) The account your servicer uses to pay taxes and insurance.
Escalation clause
An offer term that automatically beats competing offers by a set amount, up to a cap.
FAIR plan
A state-sponsored insurer of last resort for properties the regular market won't cover.
HELOC
Home equity line of credit: a revolving, usually variable-rate, second lien.
Homestead exemption
A state property-tax reduction or protection for a primary residence.
LLPA
Loan-level price adjustment: Fannie and Freddie pricing add-ons for credit score, LTV, occupancy and more.
Loan Estimate (LE)
The standardized 3-page quote a lender must give within 3 business days of application.
LTV / CLTV
Loan-to-value / combined loan-to-value (all liens).
PITI
Principal, interest, taxes and insurance: the full housing payment.
PMI / MIP
Private mortgage insurance (conventional) or mortgage insurance premium (FHA), protecting the lender on low-down-payment loans.
Points
Upfront interest; each point is 1% of the loan amount.
Rate lock
A lender's commitment to a rate and price for a set period.
Recording
Filing the deed and mortgage with the county, which makes the transfer official.
Reserves
Liquid assets left after closing, measured in months of PITI.
ROV
Reconsideration of value: a formal request to revisit an appraisal with better data.
Seller concession
Money the seller credits toward your costs, capped by loan type and occupancy.
Title insurance
A one-time premium covering defects in ownership history. The lender's policy protects the lender, and a separate owner's policy protects you.
TRID
The federal "Know Before You Owe" disclosure rule behind the LE and CD.
Wire fraud
Diversion of closing funds using spoofed instructions. Always verify by phone.
Final word from your coach

Three things to remember: shop the money as hard as the house, protect your exits (contingencies and deadlines), and verify every wire. Do those three and the rest is paperwork.
