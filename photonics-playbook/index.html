Photonics: moving and computing with light

AI clusters have hit a wall that has nothing to do with how fast a GPU can do math. The wall is moving data between chips. Light is how the industry is climbing over it, and that shift is creating a market, a supply crunch and a sourcing problem all at once.

Coach's opening note

You already know this market from the buyer's side without calling it photonics. Every 400G or 800G transceiver line on a hyperscaler or network bill, every "optics" SKU on a switch quote, every lead-time escalation on a cluster build: that is photonics. This guide gives you the physics in plain English, the map of who makes what, where the chokepoints are, and where someone with a sourcing background can make money.

The mental model: three businesses, three clocks

Now · revenue today

Move data with light

Optical interconnect: transceivers, co-packaged optics, optical switches, fiber. Billions in annual sales, growing fast, supply constrained.

Next · 2028 and beyond

Compute with light

Optical processors that do the matrix math of AI in light. Promising lab results, well-funded startups, no volume product yet.

Later · 2030s

Quantum with light

Photonic quantum computers. One public company, several unicorns, fault tolerance still unproven.

Analogy: the highway, the engine, the spaceship

Interconnect is the highway between factories: unglamorous, already congested, and paid for by everyone who ships anything. Optical compute is a new kind of engine that might be far more efficient, if anyone can build one that runs reliably. Photonic quantum is a spaceship. If you are deciding where to spend time and money, the highway is where the traffic is.

Foundations

Photonics 101: just enough physics

You do not need a physics degree to follow this market. You need to know five components, three materials and one number: energy per bit.

Why light beats copper

Electrons in copperPhotons in glass
DistanceSignal degrades fast. At 200 Gb/s per lane, passive copper reaches only a couple of meters.Barely degrades over meters, kilometers, even oceans.
Bandwidth per wireOne signal per wire pair.Many colors (wavelengths) share one fiber, each carrying its own signal.

InterferenceWires crosstalk and pick up noise.Light beams pass through each other undisturbed.

Analogy: shouting down a hallway versus a flashlight

Copper is shouting: fine across a room, useless across a building, and everyone in the hallway hears everyone else. Fiber is a flashlight with Morse code: it carries for miles, and you can run a dozen colored flashlights down the same hallway without confusion. The catch is that someone has to turn your words into flashes and back again at each end. That translation step is where most of the cost, power and failure in photonics lives.

The five parts of every optical link

1. Laser

Makes the light. Almost always made of indium phosphide (InP) or, for short links, gallium arsenide VCSELs. Silicon cannot make light efficiently, which is why lasers are the industry's biggest chokepoint.

2. Modulator

Encodes data onto the light by switching or shaping it billions of times a second. Can be built in silicon, InP, or thin-film lithium niobate. Speed and power of the modulator set the link's performance.

3. Waveguide and fiber

Carries the light: tiny waveguides etched on a chip, then optical fiber between chips, racks and buildings. Attaching fiber to a chip precisely and cheaply is a surprisingly hard manufacturing step.

4. Photodetector

Turns light back into an electrical signal at the far end. Usually germanium on silicon. The least troublesome part.

5. DSP and driver electronics

The electronic chips that clean up, drive and decode the signal. A big share of a transceiver's power and cost. Marvell, Broadcom and Credo dominate. Removing or shrinking the DSP is the whole idea behind LPO and CPO.

Put together: the transceiver

A pluggable transceiver is all five in a module the size of a pack of gum that slides into a switch port. Hyperscalers buy them by the million. This is the product line that dominates photonics revenue today.

The one number: energy per bit

Everything in data-center photonics is ultimately judged in picojoules per bit (pJ/bit): how much energy it takes to move one bit. Lower is better. A product that moves the same data for less power lets the operator put more GPUs inside the same power envelope, and power, not floor space, is what limits AI data centers now.

Link power (watts) = energy per bit (pJ/bit) × data rate (bits/s) × 10⁻¹²

Example: a 1.6 Tb/s link at 15 pJ/bit burns 24 watts. Cut it to 5 pJ/bit and it burns 8 watts. Across hundreds of thousands of links, that difference is measured in megawatts.

Coach's note

When a vendor pitches you a photonics product, three questions cut through most of the marketing: What is the pJ/bit, measured how? Where is the laser, and can I replace it? Who else can make this, in what volume? The first is performance, the second is reliability and serviceability, and the third is your supply risk.

Foundations

Why now: the I/O wall

Photonics has been "about to change computing" for twenty years. What finally changed is AI: models too big for one chip, clusters of hundreds of thousands of GPUs, and a power bill that makes every wasted watt a competitive problem.

Three forces converging

1. Copper is running out of reach

Each time signaling speed doubles, the distance copper can carry it shrinks. At 200 Gb/s per lane, passive copper is limited to a couple of meters, roughly one rack. Anything farther needs optics.

2. GPUs must act as one machine

Training a frontier model means thousands of GPUs exchanging data constantly. The bigger the "scale-up" domain that behaves like one giant GPU, the better. That domain is now outgrowing a single rack.

3. Power is the binding constraint

Data centers are capped by grid connections, not land. Optics that burn less power free up megawatts for compute. That is why Nvidia, Broadcom and every hyperscaler care.

Analogy: the factory and the forklifts

A GPU is a machine in a factory. For years the industry bought faster machines. Now the machines sit idle waiting for forklifts to bring parts. Optics are better forklifts. When buyers spend a growing slice of capex on forklifts, it is because the machines are already fast enough and the waiting is what costs money.

Watch out: this is a derivative of AI capex

Almost all of the near-term growth depends on hyperscaler AI spending. LightCounting already expects growth to moderate from 2027, with the possibility of a flat quarter or two as shortages ease. If AI capex pauses, optics orders fall faster than GPU orders, because customers burn through inventory first. Any business plan here needs to survive a down year.

The market

The interconnect ladder: from pluggables to optics on the chip

The whole market is one long migration: moving the conversion from electrical to optical closer and closer to the processor. Each rung saves power and adds integration risk.

Analogy: the plug-in GPS versus the built-in nav

A pluggable is the GPS you stick on the dashboard: any brand works, swap it when it breaks, but it uses its own power and cables. CPO is the navigation built into the dashboard: cleaner, cheaper to run, better integrated, and when it fails, it is a dealer visit and you are on the carmaker's upgrade schedule. Most fleets will run both for years.

Speed generations: the treadmill

2023-2025

800G pluggables everywhere

The workhorse of the first AI build-out. Shortages of InP lasers and VCSELs constrained shipments from 2023 through 2025.

2025-2026

1.6T ramps; 200 Gb/s per lane

Innolight's Q1 2026 revenue grew 207% year over year on 1.6T shipments. First CPO switches built on 200G lanes ship.

2027-2028

3.2T and 400 Gb/s per lane

Broadcom's fourth-generation CPO targets 400G per channel. TFLN and plasmonic modulators (Marvell bought Polariton for this) compete to get there efficiently.

2028+

Optics inside the scale-up domain

Nvidia's Rubin Ultra NVL576 brings optics, including optical circuit switching, into rack-to-rack scale-up, with volume expected from 2028.

Coach's note

The buyer's real decision is not "pluggable or CPO" in the abstract. It is which tier, which generation, and how much lock-in you will accept for how much power savings. Expect most enterprise and neocloud buyers to stay on pluggables for scale-out through 2027, with CPO appearing first where they buy a whole Nvidia or Broadcom-based reference design.

The market

Co-packaged optics: the 2026 inflection

After a decade of skepticism, CPO switches from Nvidia and Broadcom are shipping. Whether CPO becomes the default or stays a niche depends mostly on one question buyers care about more than physics: what happens when something breaks.

The two playbooks

Nvidia: CPO as part of the AI factory

  • Quantum-X Photonics (InfiniBand): 115 Tb/s, 144 ports of 800G, liquid cooled. Shipping H1 2026.
  • Spectrum-X Photonics (Ethernet): SN6810 at 102 Tb/s and SN6800 at 409 Tb/s (512 ports of 800G). H2 2026.
  • Claims up to 5x lower optics power, 10x network resiliency, 13x laser reliability (company figures).
  • Early adopters named: CoreWeave, Lambda, TACC.
  • Supply partners: TSMC, Coherent, Lumentum, Corning, Foxconn, SENKO.

Broadcom: CPO as better Ethernet

  • Tomahawk 6-Davisson: 102.4 Tb/s, 200G per lane, Broadcom's third CPO generation, after TH5-Bailly. Sampling from October 2025.
  • Claims about 70% lower optical interconnect power than pluggables.
  • Field-replaceable external laser modules (ELSFP).
  • Meta testing: over one million 400G-equivalent port hours with no link flaps on the prior generation.
  • Fourth generation in development at 400G per lane.

Both rely on TSMC's COUPE (Compact Universal Photonic Engine), which stacks an electronic chip on a photonic chip using SoIC-X 3D packaging. TSMC packaging capacity is therefore a shared dependency.

The reliability argument, both directions

Why CPO could be more reliable

Fewer connectors and fewer separate modules mean fewer things to wiggle loose or wear out. Lasers, the part most likely to fail, sit in a replaceable module away from the hot switch chip. Broadcom and Micas Networks report millions of flap-free port hours in testing.

Why buyers still hesitate

An optical engine failure on a CPO switch may mean replacing the switch or a whole tray. Operations teams know how to swap pluggables; CPO needs new spares strategies, new tooling, and trust in vendor failure data that is still mostly vendor-reported.

The interop question

With pluggables, optics from many vendors work in one switch. With CPO, the optics are the switch vendor's. That concentrates supply on fewer companies.

The upgrade question

Pluggables let you upgrade optics independently. CPO ties the optics refresh to the switch refresh, which suits hyperscalers who replace whole systems on a cycle, and suits enterprises less.

Analogy: soldered RAM in a laptop

Soldered memory makes laptops thinner, faster and cheaper to build, and it also means you can never upgrade or swap a bad stick. Most consumers accepted that trade. Fleet managers with ten thousand machines thought harder. CPO is the same trade for data-center networking, and hyperscalers are the fleet managers.

Coach's note

If you advise a buyer evaluating CPO switches, the contract is where the reliability debate gets settled. Ask for failure-rate commitments on the optical engines, on-site spares of laser modules at the vendor's cost, a defined remedy if the engine fails (tray swap, not a ticket), and field data from deployments at least as large as yours. Vendors who believe their reliability claims will sign up to them.

The market

Optical switching and fiber: the quieter growth stories

Two adjacent markets get less attention than CPO but matter just as much to data-center economics: switches that route light without converting it, and fiber that moves light faster.

Optical circuit switches (OCS)

A normal network switch converts light to electrons, decides where each packet goes, and converts back. An optical circuit switch skips all that: tiny mirrors or other elements physically redirect beams of light from one fiber to another. It cannot inspect packets, but it uses a fraction of the power, does not care about data rate, and survives several speed generations.

Analogy: a railroad switchyard

A packet switch is a sorting facility that opens every box. An OCS is a railroad switchyard that throws a lever and routes the whole train. It is slower to reconfigure, but it never has to unload anything, and it works for any train speed.

Fiber itself is changing

Hollow-core fiber

Light travels through air in the fiber's center rather than glass, which cuts latency by about a third and lowers signal loss. Microsoft planned 15,000 km across Azure and has partnered with Corning and Heraeus Covantis to scale production. For latency-sensitive links between data centers, this can stretch how far apart sites can be and still act as one cluster.

Fiber count explosion

AI data halls need many times the fiber of a cloud data hall. Corning, connector makers like SENKO and Huber+Suhner, and fiber-management vendors are seeing record demand. Unglamorous, high-volume, and easy to underestimate in a cluster budget.

Coherent optics for scale-across

When a training job spans two or more buildings or campuses, links run kilometers to tens of kilometers. That is the domain of coherent pluggables (400ZR, 800ZR/ZR+), which encode data in the light's phase and polarization as well as its brightness. LightCounting reports cloud companies cannot get enough 800ZR/ZR+ modules. Ciena, Cisco (Acacia), Marvell and Nokia lead.

Coach's note

Cluster bills of materials routinely undercount fiber, connectors, patching and OCS. These categories are commodity-looking but lead-time sensitive. A sourcing lead who locks fiber and connectivity early, with the same rigor as GPUs, prevents the classic "GPUs arrived, cables did not" delay.

The market

Optical compute: doing the math in light

The most exciting idea in the field and the furthest from revenue. Light can perform the core operation of neural networks almost for free. The hard part is everything around that operation.

How it works, in one paragraph

Neural networks spend most of their time multiplying large grids of numbers together (matrix multiplication). Encode the numbers as the brightness or phase of light, pass the light through a mesh of tunable elements, and interference does the multiplying and adding as the light travels. The answer arrives at the speed of light, and the passive optics burn almost no energy.

Analogy: the prism versus the calculator

A prism splits white light into colors instantly, with no power, because the physics does the work. An optical processor is a very elaborate, programmable prism that "splits" input data into an answer. The problem is that you have to type the question into the prism (convert digital data into light) and read the answer back out (convert light into digital), and those two steps are slow, power hungry and imprecise.

The hard problems

Conversion overhead

Data starts and ends in electronics. The digital-to-analog and analog-to-digital converters at each end can eat most of the energy the optics saved.

Precision

Optics is analog. Noise and drift limit precision to roughly what low-bit AI inference can tolerate, which narrows the use cases.

Memory and nonlinearity

Light cannot easily be stored, and the nonlinear steps of a neural network are hard to do optically. Most designs bounce back to electronics between layers.

Moving target

GPUs improve every year. An optical chip has to beat the GPU that will exist when it ships, with a software stack developers will actually use.

Watch out: performance claims

"100x faster than a GPU" usually compares the optical core alone against a full GPU, at low precision, on a hand-picked workload, before conversion overhead. Ask what end-to-end tokens per second per watt looks like on a real model, at what precision, against which GPU generation. If the answer is a simulation, the product is still a research project.

Coach's note

Treat optical compute as an option, not a plan. The earliest real business here is likely in specialist niches (encrypted computing, signal processing, scientific HPC centers like Jülich) before general AI inference. If you track one milestone, track Neurophos and Q.ANT shipping systems to paying customers and publishing independent benchmarks.

The market

Photonic quantum: the long bet

Of all the ways to build a quantum computer, photons have one big practical advantage: they can be made in ordinary semiconductor fabs and mostly run at room temperature. They also have one big problem: photons are hard to make on demand and easy to lose.

Why photons for quantum

Advantages

  • Manufactured with existing chip and photonics fabs (PsiQuantum builds with GlobalFoundries)
  • Room-temperature operation for much of the system
  • Naturally networkable over fiber

Challenges

  • Reliable single-photon sources and detectors
  • Every bit of loss in a chip or fiber destroys information
  • Error correction at the scale needed for useful work is still unproven

Coach's note

For your purposes, quantum matters mainly as a demand driver for the same supply chain: low-loss photonic chips, single-photon detectors, cryogenic and laser components, specialty fiber. A supplier qualified for quantum photonics is often also qualified for data-center photonics. That overlap is a useful lens for the supply-chain business ideas later.

Analogy: the hourglass

The chain is an hourglass. Lots of raw-material sources at the top and lots of buyers at the bottom, squeezed through a narrow waist of a few laser fabs and a few packaging lines. Whoever controls the waist sets terms for everyone above and below. In 2026 the waist is lasers and packaging.

Coach's note

Use a Kraljic lens. Lasers and CPO engines are bottleneck and strategic items: secure supply, accept price, build relationships, consider capacity reservations. Fiber, connectors and commodity pluggables are closer to leverage items: compete them, multi-source, negotiate hard. Mixing those strategies up is the most common mistake buyers make in this category.

Chokepoints and risk

The defining fact of photonics in 2026 is scarcity. Demand jumped from telecom volumes to AI volumes in about three years, and the supply chain beneath it moves on a five-year clock.

Chokepoint 1: indium phosphide lasers

Lumentum's CEO said in July 2026 that the InP shortage will be worse than the recent memory shortage: telecom customers once bought lasers in the hundreds, AI customers now want hundreds of millions. Coherent's 6-inch InP line yields more than four times the devices of a 3-inch line at under half the cost, and it expects to more than double capacity again by the end of 2027. Analysts quoted by TrendForce expect the imbalance to last beyond 2027. LightCounting is more optimistic about transceivers specifically, expecting shortages to ease by late 2026.

Watch out: who is telling you about the shortage

Laser makers' valuations depend on the shortage persisting, and analysts often echo them. Buyers' interest is the opposite. The truth for planning purposes: assume constrained laser supply through 2027 and plan allocation protection, but do not sign long take-or-pay commitments at peak prices without price-adjustment or step-down terms.

Chokepoint 2: China, on both ends

Upstream: materials

China placed indium under export controls in February 2025, and US data show unwrought indium exports fell 72% year over year to September 2025. InP substrates from AXT's China subsidiary needed export permits. Substrate wafers for the Nvidia-funded fabs are not made in the US in meaningful volume.

Downstream: modules

The world's two largest optical transceiver vendors are Chinese: Innolight ($5.3B revenue in 2025, up 61%) and Eoptolink ($3.5B, up 189%). Accelink is fourth. Most US and Japanese module makers exited by 2020. US hyperscalers rely heavily on these suppliers, many with production outside China.

Indium data: USGS via Tom's Hardware, July 2026. Module rankings: LightCounting, May 2026.

Chokepoint 3: advanced packaging

Both Nvidia and Broadcom CPO run through TSMC's COUPE packaging, which competes for the same advanced-packaging capacity as GPUs and HBM memory. Outside TSMC, ASE and Amkor are the main alternatives. Nvidia has made a $1.5B prepayment to Amkor, which has expanded its Peoria, Arizona campus plan to about $12B.

Analogy: buying the farm to guarantee the harvest

When a restaurant chain gets big enough, it stops trusting the produce market and starts buying farms or prepaying growers. Nvidia's $4B into laser makers and $1.5B to Amkor is the chain buying the farm. Every smaller buyer now competes for what is left at the produce market.

Coach's note

Three patterns to remember. First, DSP and networking chip companies (Marvell, Credo, Ciena, AMD) are buying photonics so they are not displaced by CPO. Second, startups focused purely on interconnect are getting acquired at billions; optical compute startups are still raising Series A. Third, prepayments and capacity rights are now normal in this category, which tells you what smaller buyers will be asked for.

Coach's note

Run the defaults and notice the shape of the answer: the energy bill savings are modest next to a cluster's cost, but the megawatts freed translate into hundreds of additional GPUs inside a fixed grid connection. That reframes CPO from a cost decision into a capacity decision, and it is the argument vendors will make. Your job is to price the lock-in and reliability risk against it.

Contract terms that matter in this category

  • Allocation protection: a committed share of supply during shortage, not just "best efforts."
  • Lead-time commitments with remedies, tied to your forecast cadence.
  • Price adjustment both ways: indexing or step-downs as shortages ease, so peak pricing is not locked for years.
  • Volume flexibility: the right to reduce or defer if AI capex slows.
  • Reliability and failure-rate commitments (FIT rates, link flap metrics) with credits or replacements.
  • Field-replaceable laser spares held on site, vendor funded, for CPO.
  • Technology refresh rights: move commitments to the next generation (1.6T to 3.2T, pluggable to CPO) without penalty.
  • Interoperability and MSA compliance for pluggables, and no "vendor-coded optics only" restrictions without price protection.
  • Country-of-origin disclosure by tier, plus tariff pass-through caps and export-control contingency plans.
  • Last-time-buy and end-of-life notice long enough to redesign.
Watch out: vendor-coded optics

Switch vendors have long locked their gear to their own branded optics, at large markups over the same modules sold by the original manufacturer. CPO extends that lock-in by design. When a vendor's CPO pitch includes "and you no longer need to buy transceivers," model what you currently pay for vendor-coded pluggables. Sometimes CPO is cheaper; sometimes the markup simply moved into the switch price.

Business opportunities for a sourcing operator

Building photonic chips takes hundreds of millions of dollars and PhD teams. The buyers of photonics, and the startups trying to reach volume, have a different gap: commercial and supply-chain expertise. That gap is where an experienced sourcing leader fits.

Four options, compared

1. AI networking sourcing advisory

The offer: help buyers of AI clusters source the network well. Pluggable versus CPO decisions with TCO models, vendor selection across Nvidia's integrated stack and open Ethernet ecosystems, allocation and lead-time strategy during the laser shortage, contract terms from section 11, and multi-sourcing plans that avoid single-vendor lock-in.

Best clients: neoclouds and GPU-as-a-service providers, colocation operators building AI halls, sovereign and national AI projects, universities and HPC centers, and large enterprises building private AI clusters. Hyperscalers mostly do this in house.

Why it works: hyperscaler and AI contracting experience transfers directly, and very few advisors combine commercial depth with fluency in this technology.

Risk: overlaps with what an employer consulting firm sells. See the conflict note below.

2. Supplier qualification and supply-chain risk service

The offer: audit and qualify photonics suppliers (lasers, substrates, fiber attach, packaging, module assembly), map tier-2 and tier-3 concentration and country-of-origin risk, and support second-source development and reshoring decisions.

Best clients: photonics companies needing qualified second sources, module makers diversifying away from China-linked inputs, and large buyers who must show supply-chain resilience to boards or government programs.

Why it works: aerospace-grade supplier management and cyber-risk programs across hundreds of suppliers are rare in a market that grew up selling to telecom. It scales with hired auditors and repeatable methods, and Arizona's semiconductor cluster is on the doorstep.

3. Defense and government go-to-market

The offer: help photonics startups sell to the Defense Department and civil agencies: SBIR/STTR strategy, prime-contractor partnering, ITAR and export-compliance readiness, and contracting vehicles.

Why the demand exists: photonics fits defense needs (secure communications, low-power edge processing, sensing, quantum networking), and government money is already present in the sector, from CHIPS funding to the UK national security fund in Optalysys.

Why it works: West Point, Army officer service and Boeing's defense exposure give credibility most commercial consultants lack.

4. Fractional procurement leadership

The offer: serve as part-time head of procurement for three or four funded photonics or AI hardware startups: foundry and packaging agreements, capacity reservations, long-term supply agreements, contract manufacturing, and building the first procurement processes.

Why it works: startups like Neurophos (building toward first systems in 2028) or a Series B interconnect company face the exact capacity fights Nvidia is winning with prepayments, with a fraction of the leverage. Compensation often includes equity, giving exposure to the sector without investing personal capital.

Other ideas worth a look

  • Optics spares and lifecycle management for neoclouds: forecasting failures, managing RMAs, holding spares pools across customers.
  • Secondary market for optics: testing, recertifying and reselling transceivers as clusters upgrade generations. Shortages make this more valuable.
  • Benchmarking data: anonymized pricing, lead-time and contract-term benchmarks for AI networking, sold by subscription. Hard to start, very sticky once it exists.
  • Content and community: a newsletter or briefing on AI networking procurement builds the audience for any of the above at almost no cost.

Days 1-30

Days 31-60

Analogy: selling shovels in a gold rush

The classic advice is to sell shovels. Photonics companies are the shovel sellers of the AI gold rush, and the market knows it, so shovel stocks are priced like gold mines. Glossary, watch list and sources

The vocabulary you will hear in vendor briefings, the dates that will tell you how the market is going, and where every figure in this guide came from.

Glossary

800G / 1.6T / 3.2T
Transceiver speeds in gigabits or terabits per second. Each generation doubles bandwidth per port.
CPO (co-packaged optics)
Optical engines in the same package as a switch or processor chip, removing most of the copper path and the pluggable module.
COUPE
TSMC's Compact Universal Photonic Engine, the 3D packaging platform under Nvidia's and Broadcom's CPO.
Coherent optics
Longer-reach optics that encode data in phase and polarization; used between data centers (400ZR, 800ZR).
DSP
Digital signal processor inside a transceiver that cleans and decodes the signal; a large share of module power.
ELSFP
External laser small form-factor pluggable: a replaceable laser module that feeds light to CPO engines.
EML
Electro-absorption modulated laser, a common high-speed InP laser in datacom; one of the scarcest parts in 2026.
Hollow-core fiber
Fiber that guides light through air, cutting latency by about a third.
InP (indium phosphide)
The semiconductor that makes datacom lasers. Its supply is the industry's main bottleneck.
Link flap
A link briefly dropping and reconnecting; disruptive in AI training jobs and a key reliability metric for optics.
LPO / LRO
Linear pluggable optics / linear receive optics: pluggables that remove all or part of the DSP to save power.
MSA
Multi-source agreement: an industry spec that lets many vendors make interchangeable modules.
NPO
Near-packaged optics: optical engines on the board close to the chip, a step short of CPO.
OCS
Optical circuit switch: redirects light between fibers without converting to electrical signals.
Optical I/O chiplet
A small optical chip packaged next to a GPU or accelerator to give it optical connections directly.
pJ/bit
Picojoules per bit, the efficiency yardstick for interconnect.
Scale-up / scale-out / scale-across
GPUs within a tightly coupled domain / racks across a data hall / data centers across a region.
SerDes
Serializer/deserializer, the circuit that sends high-speed signals off a chip; 200G per lane is current, 400G is next.
SiPho (silicon photonics)
Optical components built in standard silicon chip fabs.
TFLN
Thin-film lithium niobate, a material for very fast, efficient modulators.
VCSEL
Vertical-cavity surface-emitting laser, a cheap GaAs laser for short links.

Cheat sheet

  • Three businesses: move data (now), compute with light (next), quantum (later).
  • The constraint is moving data and power, not GPU math.
  • The migration: pluggable → LPO → NPO/CPO → optical I/O on the chip.
  • The chokepoints: InP lasers, advanced packaging, and China at both ends.
  • The pattern: chip companies buy photonics; Nvidia prepays suppliers.
  • The buyer's questions: pJ/bit, where is the laser, who else can make it.
  • The opportunity for a sourcing operator: buyers and startups need commercial and supply-chain expertise more than physics.
